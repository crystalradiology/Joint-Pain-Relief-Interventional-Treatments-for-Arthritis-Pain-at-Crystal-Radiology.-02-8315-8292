What Are Interventional Treatments?
Interventional treatments are minimally invasive procedures used to diagnose and treat various medical conditions, including arthritis. Using imaging tools like X-rays or ultrasounds, physicians guide small instruments, such as needles or catheters, to deliver medication directly to the source of pain. These treatments are a preferred alternative to traditional surgery because they are less invasive, require minimal recovery time, and can often be performed on an outpatient basis.
Top Interventional Treatments for Arthritis Pain
If you are seeking effective joint pain relief, here’s a comprehensive look at some of the leading interventional treatments available:
1. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) Therapy
PRP therapy uses a patient’s blood to promote tissue repair and reduce inflammation. Blood is drawn and spun in a centrifuge to isolate platelets, which are rich in growth factors. These concentrated platelets are then injected into the affected joint, stimulating natural healing.
2. Joint Injections
One of the most commonly used interventional treatments, joint injections deliver a combination of anesthetic and anti-inflammatory medication directly into the joint. These injections can provide immediate relief, reducing pain and inflammation, and allowing patients to return to their daily activities more comfortably.
3. Nerve Blocks
Nerve blocks involve injecting a numbing agent or steroid near specific nerves to temporarily disrupt pain signals. This treatment is particularly effective for arthritis pain in the spine, hips, shoulders, or knees. Nerve blocks can be repeated as needed to provide ongoing relief.
4. Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)
RFA is a minimally invasive procedure that uses heat generated by radio waves to disrupt pain signals from affected nerves. Often used for neck, back, or knee osteoarthritis, RFA can offer long-term relief lasting a year or more.
5. Prolotherapy
Prolotherapy involves injecting a natural irritant solution into the affected joint to stimulate the body’s healing processes. This treatment strengthens weakened ligaments and improves joint function, providing long-term pain relief.

Who Is at Risk for Arthritis?
Arthritis affects millions of individuals worldwide, but certain risk factors increase the likelihood of developing the condition:
•	Age: Aging increases the risk of arthritis, with over half of adults aged 65 and older experiencing osteoarthritis.
•	Genetics: A family history of arthritis can raise your risk, though it doesn’t guarantee you’ll develop the condition.
•	Gender: Women are more likely to develop arthritis, particularly autoimmune forms such as RA. Hormonal changes may play a role in joint health.
•	Obesity: Excess weight places additional stress on joints, especially the knees and hips, making osteoarthritis more likely.
•	Previous Joint Injuries: Past injuries can increase the risk of arthritis by damaging cartilage and accelerating joint wear.
•	Repetitive Strain: Occupations involving repetitive movements or heavy labor, such as construction, increase arthritis risk.
•	Autoimmune Conditions: Conditions like lupus or RA can increase susceptibility to arthritis, with symptoms often developing between ages 30 and 60.
